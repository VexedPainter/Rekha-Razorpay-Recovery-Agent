--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 1. WHAT THIS IS, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When someone tries to buy something online and the payment fails, that is lost
revenue. Often it is recoverable: the bank timed out, the customer walked away
mid-checkout, or they simply had no money until payday. Rekha reads a pile of
failed payments, works out WHY each one failed, decides which are worth chasing
and how, and then chases them — but only inside strict limits it cannot widen.

The name is Hindi/Sanskrit for "the line". From "Lakshman Rekha": a boundary you
do not cross. That is the whole idea.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 2. WHY IT IS BUILT THE WAY IT IS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

An AI good enough to decide "this failed payment is worth ₹4,200 of chasing" is
also capable of being confidently wrong, or of being manipulated by text a
customer typed into an order note.

So the system is split in two halves with a hard line between them:

  THE THINKING HALF   (recovery/)
  Talks to the AI. Reads failed payments, guesses causes, proposes what to do.
  It can propose ANYTHING. It is treated as untrusted.

  THE DECIDING HALF   (rekha/)
  Never talks to an AI. Takes each proposal and checks it against the merchant's
  written permissions, spending caps, contact limits, and — above a threshold —
  a human. Then executes, and writes down what happened in a tamper-evident log.

The AI proposes. It never decides what it is allowed to do.

Enforced mechanically, not by good intentions: a test reads the source code and
fails the build if the thinking half imports anything from the deciding half's
ledger, approvals, policy, executor, or mandate. An agent that can write its own
evidence could rewrite its own track record.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 3. THE FIVE-MINUTE VERSION FOR THE VIDEO
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  1. Here are 200 real-shaped failed payments. ₹6,55,134 at risk.
  2. The AI reads them all in 20 batched calls and classifies why each failed.
     Measured against ground truth on a held-out set: 100% macro F1, versus 93%
     for a twelve-line keyword rule table. The rule table was included expecting
     it might win.
  3. For each payment it proposes a SEQUENCE, not one action: wait, then a UPI
     link, then a reminder, then stop.
  4. The control plane refuses anything outside the mandate, pauses anything
     above the approval threshold, and never lets two live payment links exist
     for one order — because then the customer can pay twice.
  5. Webhooks come back. Money recovered is a MEASURED fact, not a claim.
  6. Then settlement verification: did the money that actually landed match what
     we authorised? Inject a fault and it exits non-zero.

  The thing to say out loud: sequencing recovers 79% more money by making 81%
  more contacts. The gain is persistence, not brilliance. What is hard — and
  what this project actually builds — is making persistence SAFE.

 4. RUN IT YOURSELF
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Open PowerShell in this folder.

  # one-time setup
  uv venv
  uv pip install -e .

  # THE MAIN DEMO — this is what to record
  python examples\demo_recovery.py

You do NOT need an API key. Real Gemini output for all 200 payments is recorded
in recovery/fixtures/, so the demo replays genuine model reasoning offline and
gives the same answer every time.

Other things worth showing:

  rekha bench run
      Seven attacks, including a prompt injection hidden in a customer's order
      note. Reports the block rate AND the false-positive rate — a system that
      refuses everything would score 100% on the first number alone.

  rekha bench evaluate
      Diagnosis accuracy against ground truth the AI never saw, versus two
      baselines.

  rekha bench backtest --sweep
      What sequencing earns, shown across the range of one assumption.

  rekha-conformance run --target rekha --level 3
      The specification suite, top level.

If you want to use a live model instead of the recordings, put a free Gemini or
Groq key in .env and add --provider gemini.

 5. THE NUMBERS, AND THE ONES THAT ARE UNFLATTERING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Say these plainly. They are the strongest part of the submission, because
anybody can claim the flattering ones.

  GOOD
    100% macro F1 on held-out diagnosis, vs 93% for a keyword baseline
    100% of 7 attacks blocked, 0 false positives on 10 legitimate recoveries
    717 tests, 84% branch coverage, L3 conformance, 31 spec MUSTs covered
    Sequencing: +79.3% money recovered

  HONEST CAVEATS — VOLUNTEER THESE
    The +79.3% rests on a guess about how much customers resent being chased.
    At the pessimistic end it is +38%, at the optimistic end +115%. Quote the
    RANGE. It is printed in every report and swept with --sweep.

    Efficiency per contact does NOT improve (₹832 → ₹823). Sequencing is
    persistence, not intelligence.

    The AI's probability estimates are BADLY CALIBRATED — about 18 points too
    pessimistic, and they lose to simply quoting the average. But they RANK
    correctly, and ranking is the only thing they are used for. Different
    question, and the report explains the difference rather than hiding the bad
    number.

    An earlier version of that same measurement, on 5 outcomes, said the
    forecasts were GOOD (+9.6%). With 196 outcomes it says they are bad (-6.7%).
    The small sample did not just lack precision — it pointed the wrong way.
    That is why the report always prints how many outcomes it could observe.

    The AI's timing judgement is narrow: only two distinct delays (24h and 48h).
    It correctly works out that insufficient-funds failures need days rather
    than hours, and nothing finer.

    The test data is synthetic. Real Razorpay error text is messier, so accuracy
    would fall. The MARGIN over the keyword baseline is what transfers.

 6. THE BEST STORIES TO TELL A PANEL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Pick two or three. Each one shows a bug found by thinking about money rather
than about agents.

  TWO LIVE LINKS = PAYING TWICE
  If step 2 sends a UPI link and step 3 sends a card link, the customer holds
  two valid demands for one order and can pay both. The merchant refunds one
  having paid fees on both. So escalating the payment method now REQUIRES
  cancelling the live link first, and a "remind" action exists specifically so
  the common second nudge creates no new obligation at all.

  AN APPROVED PLAN IS NOT A BLANK CHEQUE
  The easy way to build multi-step recovery is: merchant approves once, agent
  acts four times over a week. But approval was granted against the situation as
  it stood. By day four the payment may be refunded, or the cap exhausted. So
  every step is re-checked against current evidence. There is deliberately NO
  fast path for an "already approved" step.

  THE BASELINE I CAUGHT MYSELF RIGGING
  The first sequencing measurement said +94.7%. A test flagged a contact-count
  mismatch: on the 13 payments where the AI says "wait", the single-shot
  baseline was doing NOTHING at all, ever — handing sequencing the entire
  recovery. A real single-shot system has no "wait" option; it sends a link
  immediately. Fixed the baseline, and the number fell to +79.3%. That number
  is worth more than the first one.

  MEASURING THE MODEL, NOT TRUSTING IT
  The AI's forecasts were never written down anywhere, so nothing could check
  them. A forecaster that is never scored is indistinguishable from a random
  one. Now every forecast goes into the ledger — written by the control plane,
  never by the AI itself — and gets scored against what actually happened.

  A 3% SCORE THAT WAS A PLUMBING BUG
  The evaluation first reported the model at 3% accuracy, which looked
  catastrophic. The cause was batching: scoring a shuffled subset changed the
  request, missed the recorded fixtures, and silently scored a fallback default.
  A plumbing mistake wearing the costume of a model failure.
